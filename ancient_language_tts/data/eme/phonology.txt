Stage. Educated London English of about 1600, the Original Pronunciation used for Shakespeare. The Great Vowel Shift has run most of its course, but not all of it: meat is not yet meet, face is a pure long vowel, price and mouth begin with a central vowel, love and good share one vowel, and r is pronounced after every vowel. The spelling looks like today's English. The values below replace today's values wherever they differ. Do not return a modern dictionary pronunciation.

Output
- Give a phonetic transcription of careful speech, one word at a time. Do not apply sound changes across word boundaries.
- Separate syllables with a dot and words with a space. Drop capitals. The pause marks are a comma, an exclamation mark, a question mark, and a period. A comma is a short pause. An exclamation mark and a question mark stay as they are. A period is a long pause and the end of a sentence. A semicolon or a colon is a long pause: write a period. Drop parentheses, dashes, and quotation marks.
- A line break in verse is a space, unless the text puts a pause mark there.

Spelling of the input
- The input may be in original spelling or modernized. Read both the same way: u and v, i and j, and a final -e are spelling, not sound. vnto is unto, iust is just, doe is do, beene is been.
- An apostrophe marks a lost syllable, and the syllable is not pronounced: lov'd [ˈlʊvd], heav'n [ˈhɛvn] is one syllable, o'er [ˈoːɹ], e'en [ˈiːn], 'tis [ˈtɪz], th' [ð].
- A grave or acute accent on -èd or -éd marks a syllable: belovèd [bɪˈlʊ.vɪd], cursèd [ˈkəɹ.sɪd].
- y at the end of a word is a vowel. y at the start of a word before a vowel is [j]: yet, yonder, you.

Vowels, by the lexical set the word belongs to
- KIT [ɪ]: sit, wind, his, busy.
- DRESS [ɛ]: bed, head, dead, breath, any. Words with modern ea and a short vowel are DRESS.
- TRAP [a]: man, cat, hand, shall. BATH is TRAP: path, last, after, dance [ˈdans]. There is no trap–bath split.
- a after w or wh is still TRAP. The rounding of was, what, want, wash, and swan has not happened: what [ˈʍat], was [ˈwaz], want [ˈwant].
- LOT [ɔ]: not, hot, God. CLOTH is LOT, short: soft [ˈsɔft], cross, off, often, lost.
- STRUT and FOOT are one vowel [ʊ]. They have not split: love [ˈlʊv], cut, sun, come, blood [ˈblʊd], good, foot, put, book. Words with oo and a modern short vowel are [ʊ].
- FLEECE [iː]: see, be, me, he, she, we, thee, meet, green, piece, field. These are the words with modern ee or ie, and the pronouns.
- The MEAT set [eː]: meat, sea, speak, east, reason, please, lease, beast, dream, peace. These are the words with modern ea and a long vowel, and their families. They have the vowel of FACE, not of FLEECE. great, break, and steak are in this set too. When you cannot tell whether a word with modern [iː] belongs to FLEECE or to MEAT, use [iː].
- FACE [eː]: name, made, face, nation, and day, way, say, pay, rain, vein, they. The ai, ay, ei, ey words have merged with name.
- PALM [a]: father, palm [ˈpam], calm, alms. It is TRAP, with no l.
- THOUGHT [ɔː]: all, law, walk [ˈwɔːk], talk, caught, daughter, saw, cause.
- GOAT [oː]: go, so, home, stone, noble, know, grow, soul, own, window [ˈwɪn.doː], though. The o words and the ow words have merged.
- GOOSE [uː]: food, moon, do, too, who, prove, move, lose, soon, through.
- The due set [ɪu]: due [ˈdɪu], new, few, dew, tune, duke, music, Jupiter, Juliet, true, rude. Words with modern [juː] or [uː] after a consonant from French u, ew, eu, or ue have [ɪu].
- PRICE [əɪ]: time, I, my, eye, night, fire, wise, kind, wind in rhyme with kind.
- MOUTH [əʊ]: now, how, out, house, our, hour, thou, sound.
- CHOICE has two values. oi in join, point, boil, poison, voice, and noise is [əɪ], the vowel of PRICE: join [ˈdʒəɪn]. oy in boy, joy, toy, coy, and employ is [ɔɪ].
- happY, the final y or ie of a word, is [ɪ]: lovely [ˈlʊv.lɪ], merry, city. In rhyme with a PRICE word, as in Sonnet rhymes on die, eye, or I, it is [əɪ]: memory [ˈmɛ.mə.rəɪ] rhyming with die.
- commA and every reduced unstressed vowel are [ə].

Vowels before r
- NURSE is [əɹ] whatever the spelling: bird [ˈbəɹd], word, heard, her, work, turn, earth, first.
- lettER is [əɹ]: winter [ˈwɪn.təɹ], father, honour, nature.
- NEAR [iːɹ]: here, dear, ear, fear.
- SQUARE [eːɹ]: care, there, where, compare, fair, bear, wear.
- START [aɹ]: art, far, heart, hard, park, darling.
- NORTH [ɔːɹ]: horse [ˈhɔːɹs], short, lord, for, or, born, York.
- FORCE [oːɹ], distinct from NORTH: hoarse [ˈhoːɹs], more, door, four, sworn, floor, glory.
- CURE [uːɹ]: poor, moor, sure. With the due set, [ɪuɹ]: cure, pure.
- The ending -ture is [təɹ]: nature [ˈneː.təɹ], creature, venture. -sure is [zəɹ] or [səɹ]: pleasure [ˈplɛ.zəɹ], measure.

Unstressed syllables and endings
- An unstressed short vowel is [ə]: about, compare [kəmˈpeːɹ], discontent, temperate [ˈtɛm.pə.rət].
- The prefixes be-, de-, re-, e-, and pre- are [ɪ]: beloved, delight, return [rɪˈtəɹn].
- -ing is [ɪn] when unstressed: darling [ˈdaɹ.lɪn], loving, morning.
- -ed is pronounced as a syllable [ɪd] after t and d, and where an accent or the meter shows it. Elsewhere it is [d] or [t], as in modern English: loved [ˈlʊvd], walked [ˈwɔːkt].
- -es and -est are [ɪz] and [ɪst] where they are syllables: horses [ˈhɔːɹ.sɪz], lovest.
- -tion and -sion are two syllables, [sɪ.ən] and [zɪ.ən] after a vowel, [sɪ.ən] after a consonant: nation [ˈneː.sɪ.ən], occasion [ɔˈkeː.zɪ.ən]. In verse, where the meter counts one syllable, they are [sjən], [tjən], or [zjən]: question [ˈkwɛs.tjən] at the end of a line of eleven syllables. They are never [ʃən] or [ʒən].
- In verse, an unstressed i or e before another vowel glides to [j] when the meter needs one syllable fewer: glorious [ˈgloː.rjəs], Juliet [ˈdʒɪu.ljɛt], marriage. Otherwise it is a syllable [ɪ]: glorious in prose is [ˈgloː.rɪ.əs].
- In verse, heaven, even, ever, spirit, and power are often one syllable: [ˈhɛvn], [ˈiːn], [ˈeːɹ], [ˈspɪɹt], [ˈpəʊɹ]. Use one syllable when the meter needs it, two otherwise.
- the is [ðə]. a is [ə]. an is [ən]. to is [tə] before a consonant and [tʊ] before a vowel. All other words keep their full vowel.

Consonants
- r is [ɹ] in every position where it is written, before a consonant and at the end of a word too: art [ˈaɹt], winter [ˈwɪn.təɹ].
- kn is [kn]: knight [ˈknəɪt], know. gn at the start of a word is [gn]: gnaw. wr is [wr]: write, wrong.
- wh is [ʍ], distinct from w [w]: what [ˈʍat], which, where, why. who, whom, whose, and whole are [h].
- gh is silent: night [ˈnəɪt], light, thought [ˈθɔːt], daughter, though. Where the modern word has [f] it is [f]: laugh [ˈlaf], enough, rough, cough, tough.
- h is [h], except in French loans that had no h in speech yet: hour [ˈəʊɹ], honest, honour, heir, herb, humble, humour, host, hospital.
- l is silent in walk, talk, chalk, folk, yolk, half, calf, calm, palm, balm, alms, salmon, and in could, would, should. It is [l] everywhere else.
- ng is [ŋ] at the end of a word and before a suffix: sing [ˈsɪŋ], singer [ˈsɪŋ.əɹ]. Inside a simple word before a vowel it is [ŋg]: finger [ˈfɪŋ.gəɹ], longer, stronger, anger, English.
- th is [ð] at the start of the, this, that, these, those, then, than, there, they, them, their, thee, thou, thy, thine, though, thus, and between vowels inside a word: father, other. Elsewhere it is [θ]: think, thought, breath, earth, with [ˈwɪθ], hath [ˈhaθ], doth, saith.
- s is [z] where the modern word has [z]: is [ˈɪz], his, was, as, reason. The endings -s and -es after a voiced sound are [z]: buds [ˈbʊdz], summer's.
- si, ti, ci, and ssi before an unstressed vowel follow the -tion rule: a syllable [sɪ], [zɪ], [tɪ] in prose, and [sj], [zj], [tj] where the meter needs one syllable fewer: passion [ˈpa.sɪ.ən] or [ˈpa.sjən], gracious [ˈgreː.sɪ.əs]. sure and sugar have [ʃ].
- A doubled consonant letter is one consonant. There are no geminates.
- p b t d k g f v θ ð s z ʃ ʒ tʃ dʒ m n l j w h have their modern English values. t between vowels is [t], never a tap.

Syllables
- Every vowel or diphthong makes one syllable. Syllabify each word separately.
- A single consonant between vowels goes with the following vowel. [tʃ], [dʒ], [ʃ], [ʒ], [θ], and [ð] each count as one consonant.
- Of two or more consonants, the last goes with the following vowel and the rest close the preceding syllable: winter [ˈwɪn.təɹ], darling [ˈdaɹ.lɪn].
- A stop or f plus l or r stays together and begins the next syllable: temperate [ˈtɛm.pə.rət], England [ˈɪŋ.glənd].
- A final -le, -el, -on, or -en after a consonant is a syllable with [ə]: noble [ˈnoː.bəl], reason [ˈreː.zən].
- A prefix boundary overrides these rules: dis.content, un.to.

Stress
- A monosyllable takes ˈ unless it is an article, a preposition, a conjunction, a possessive, a personal pronoun, or an auxiliary before another verb: the, a, an, of, to, in, on, at, by, with, for, from, through, and, or, but, nor, that, if, as, my, thy, his, her, our, your, their, I, thou, thee, he, she, it, we, you, me, him, us, them, shall, will, do, did, doth, have, hath, can, may, must. That same verb takes ˈ when it is the main verb: is [ˈɪz], art [ˈaɹt], hath all [ˈhaθ ˈɔːl].
- A word of more than one syllable takes the modern lexical stress, unless it is one of the words below.
- Shakespeare's stress differs from today's in these words, and the verse confirms it: aspect [aˈspɛkt], revenue [rɪˈvɛ.nɪu], persever [pəɹˈsɛ.vəɹ], obdurate [ɔbˈdɪu.rət], canonized [kaˈnɔ.nəɪzd], advertised [adˈvəɹ.təɪzd], commendable [ˈkɔ.mən.də.bəl], complete [ˈkɔm.pliːt] before a stressed noun, extreme [ˈɛk.striːm] before a stressed noun, triumphing [trəɪˈʊm.fɪn], sepulchre [sɪˈpʊl.kəɹ], contrary [kənˈtreː.rɪ], record as a verb or noun [rɪˈkɔːɹd], exile [ɛkˈsəɪl], envy as a verb [ɛnˈvəɪ].
- In verse the line is iambic: ten syllables, or eleven with an unstressed ending. Where you are not sure of a stress or a syllable count, choose the reading that keeps the line regular.
- Put ˈ before the stressed syllable. It replaces the dot.
