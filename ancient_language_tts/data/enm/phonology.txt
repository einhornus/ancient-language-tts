Stage. London Middle English of about 1400, the language of Chaucer and the Ellesmere manuscript. The Great Vowel Shift has not happened: tyme has [iː], hous has [uː], name has [aː], mone has [oː]. Every written consonant is still pronounced. The spelling often looks like modern English. Do not apply modern English values to it, and do not return a modern dictionary pronunciation.

Output
- Give a phonetic transcription of careful speech, one word at a time. Do not apply sound changes across word boundaries, except the elision of final -e described below.
- Separate syllables with a dot and words with a space. Drop capitals. The pause marks are a comma, an exclamation mark, a question mark, and a period. A comma is a short pause. An exclamation mark and a question mark stay as they are. A period is a long pause and the end of a sentence. A semicolon or a colon is a long pause: write a period. Drop parentheses and quotation marks.
- A line break in verse is a space, unless the text puts a pause mark there.

Spelling of the input
- y and i are the same vowel letter: nyght, tyme, wyf. y at the start of a word or a syllable, before a vowel, is the consonant [j]: yong, yeer, ye "you" [ˈjeː], yow. ye "eye" is the vowel [ˈiː.ə], and so is its plural yen [ˈiː.ən]. The past-participle prefix y- or i- is the vowel [ɪ]: yronne [ɪˈrʊn.nə], ycleped.
- u and v are the same letter. Between vowels, and before a vowel at the start of a word, it is the consonant [v]: loue = love, euery = every, vertu. Elsewhere it is a vowel: vp = up, vs = us.
- o is written for [ʊ] next to m, n, u, v, w: sone "son" [ˈsʊ.nə], sonne [ˈsʊn.nə], come [ˈkʊ.mə], love [ˈlʊ.və], monk, yong, tonge, wonder. Where the modern word has the vowel of cup from this source, use [ʊ].
- ȝ (yogh) is [j] at the start of a word (ȝe) and [ç] or [x] before t or at the end of a word, like gh.
- þ is th. ð is th.
- j is [dʒ]: joye, Jankyn.
- k is [k]. c is [s] before e, i, y and [k] elsewhere: certeyn, cité, cours, clene. ck is [k].
- g is [dʒ] before e, i, y in French loans: gentil, corage, age, sergeant. In native words it is [g] before every vowel: gest "guest", gyve, begynne, togidre.
- qu is [kw]. x is [k.s] between vowels and [ks] elsewhere.
- French gn inside or at the end of a word is [n], and the vowel before it is long: signe [ˈsiː.nə], digne, benigne [bɛˈniː.nə].
- A doubled vowel letter (aa, ee, oo) is one long vowel.

Long and short vowels
- A vowel written double is long.
- A single a, e, or o in an open syllable of a native or early French word, before a single consonant and another vowel, is long: name [ˈnaː.mə], make, tale, mete "meat" [ˈmɛː.tə], hope [ˈhɔː.pə], open [ˈɔː.pən].
- A single vowel before a doubled consonant or before two consonants is short: hadde [ˈhad.də], sonne, croppes, kepte [ˈkɛp.tə], dette.
- Exception, from lengthening before a homorganic group. i, y, and ou are long before ld, nd, and mb: child [ˈtʃiːld], fynde [ˈfiːn.də], clymbe, founde [ˈfuːn.də]. e is long close [eː] before ld: feld [ˈfeːld], feldes [ˈfeːl.dəs], sheeld, yelde. o is long [ɔː] before ld where the modern word has [oʊ]: olde [ˈɔːl.də], holde, colde, tolde, bolde. The lengthening does not happen when a third consonant follows (children [ˈtʃɪl.drən]), nor in wolde [ˈwɔl.də], sholde [ˈʃɔl.də], shulde [ˈʃʊl.də]. a stays short before nd and mb: hand, lamb.
- A vowel before gh keeps the length it has in the word. Short: nyght [ˈnɪçt], knyght, myght, droghte [ˈdrʊxt]. ogh and ough at the end of a word, where the modern word ends in [aʊ] or [ʌf], are long [uː]: ynogh [ɪˈnuːx], bough, plough, tough, lough "laughed" [ˈluːx]. A diphthong stays a diphthong: taughte [ˈtaux.tə], thought [ˈθɔuxt], heigh [ˈhaiç].
- i and y are long [iː] where the modern word has [aɪ]: tyme, ryde, lyf, wyf, myn, I, fynde, child, delit. They are also long in the stressed syllable of a French loan when it ends the word or is open: servyse [sɛrˈviː.sə], malice [maˈliː.sə], justice, melodye, chivalrie, curteisie. Elsewhere they are short [ɪ], also in an open syllable of a native word: with [wɪθ], his, swich, Aprille, Cristes [ˈkrɪs.təs], lymes [ˈlɪ.məs], lyvyng [ˈlɪ.vɪŋg], yiven. An i or u lengthened in an open syllable became [eː] or [oː] and is written e or o: weke "week" [ˈweː.kə], wode "wood" [ˈwoː.də], dore [ˈdoː.rə].

Vowels
- Short a [a], e [ɛ], i and y [ɪ], o [ɔ], u [ʊ]. The o of the cup words above is [ʊ].
- Long a [aː]: name, cas, fader. It is not [eɪ].
- Long e has two values. Close [eː] is the vowel of modern meet, see, sweet, green, deep, be, he, me: sweete [ˈsweː.tə], grene, slepen [ˈsleː.pən]. Open [ɛː] is the vowel of modern meat, sea, heath, breath, clean, speak, great, break, eek: heeth [ˈhɛːθ], breeth [ˈbrɛːθ], eek [ˈɛːk], clene [ˈklɛː.nə]. The spelling does not tell them apart. Use the modern reflex: a modern ee or ie, or the vowel of field, is [eː]. A modern ea, or a modern short e from a long vowel (breath, dead, deaf), is [ɛː]. When you cannot tell, use [eː].
- Long i and y [iː]: tyme, ryde, wyf. It is not [aɪ].
- Long o has two values. Close [oː] is the vowel of modern food, good, root, moon, foot, book, do, to: roote [ˈroː.tə], soote [ˈsoː.tə], goode, mone, sooth. Open [ɔː] is the vowel of modern boat, stone, home, holy, go, so, both, and of every o that came from Old English ā: hooly [ˈhɔː.lɪ], hoom, goon [ˈgɔːn], stoon, so, open. Use the modern reflex: a modern [uː] or [ʊ] is [oː], a modern [oʊ] is [ɔː]. When you cannot tell, use [oː]. It is not yet [uː].
- ou and ow in words whose modern vowel is [aʊ], and in French loans with ou, are long [uː]: hous [ˈhuːs], shoures [ˈʃuː.rəs], flour [ˈfluːr], licour [lɪˈkuːr], cours, foweles [ˈfuː.ləs], now [ˈnuː], how. So are thou [ˈðuː], yow [ˈjuː], oure [ˈuː.rə], youre, and koude, coude "could" [ˈkuː.də]. It is not [aʊ].
- Unstressed final y and ie are [ɪ]: every [ˈɛv.rɪ], hooly, Caunterbury, sely.

Diphthongs
- ai, ay, ei, ey are [ai]: day [ˈdai], wey, veyne [ˈvai.nə], seyde, certeyn [sɛrˈtain], verray [ˈvɛr.rai].
- au and aw are [au]: cause, lawe, straunge [ˈstraun.dʒə], taughte. The a before n plus a consonant in French loans is often written au and is [au].
- ou and ow in words whose modern vowel is [oʊ] or [ɔː] are [ɔu]: knowe [ˈknɔu.ə], growe, soule, thought [ˈθɔuxt], broughte, nought. This includes every ough and ought whose modern word has the vowel of law or of go.
- ew and eu are [iu] in words from Old English ēow, īw, and ēw: newe [ˈniu.ə], trewe, knew, hewe "hue", rewe "rue", chewe. They are [ɛu] in words from Old English ēaw, ǣw, and ew, and in French eau: fewe [ˈfɛu.ə], shewe, dew, lewed, beautee [bɛuˈteː]. When you cannot tell, use [iu].
- French u, and French ew and eu, are [iu], the ordinary vernacular value: vertu [vɛrˈtiu], nature [naˈtiu.rə], vertuous, mesure. Do not use the front rounded [y], which belonged at most to learned French-speaking usage.
- oi and oy are [ɔi]: joye [ˈdʒɔi.ə], coy, point.
- Each diphthong is one syllable.

Unstressed vowels and endings
- Unstressed e is [ə]. The endings -es, -ed, -en, -eth, and -er are syllables with [ə]: shoures [ˈʃuː.rəs], perced [ˈpɛr.səd], bathed [ˈbaː.ðəd], maken [ˈmaː.kən], priketh [ˈprɪ.kəθ], fader [ˈfaː.dər].
- Final -e is [ə], its own syllable: name [ˈnaː.mə], roote, soote, Aprille [aˈprɪl.lə], knowe [ˈknɔu.ə]. It is pronounced at a pause and at the end of a verse line.
- In verse, final -e before a consonant is pronounced by default. Drop it only where the line cannot otherwise have ten or eleven syllables: this happens most in unstressed forms such as hadde, were, wolde, sholde, and in a word that already ends in an unstressed syllable. Outside verse, pronounce it.
- Before a word that begins with a vowel, drop the final -e: droghte of [ˈdrʊxt ɔf], veyne in [ˈvain ɪn]. Drop it also before an h that is silent and before the weak words he, his, him, hir, hem, hath, hadde, how, here: thanne he [ˈðan ˈheː].
- The -e of the, he, me, she, we, be, ye, thee is not this -e. It is the stressed vowel of the word: [ðə] for the, [ˈheː] for he.
- In verse, an unstressed e between two consonants, next to another unstressed syllable, is often silent: every [ˈɛv.rɪ], evere [ˈɛv.rə], foweles [ˈfuː.ləs], palmeres [ˈpal.mərs], sovereyn. Drop it where the line of ten or eleven syllables needs it. Outside verse, keep it except in every and evere.
- Pronounce every other vowel. Do not elide anything the rules above do not drop.

Consonants
- Every written consonant is pronounced. kn is [kn]: knyght [ˈknɪçt], knowe. gn at the start of a word is [gn]: gnawe. wr is [wr]: write. l is [l] in folk, half, walke, palmer, shulde, wolde, colde. b is [b] after m: lamb, clymbe.
- gh is [ç] after a front vowel or a diphthong that ends in i: nyght [ˈnɪçt], knyght, myght, heigh [ˈhaiç]. It is [x] after a back vowel or a diphthong that ends in u: droghte [ˈdrʊxt], thought [ˈθɔuxt], taughte [ˈtaux.tə], lough. It is never silent and never [f].
- h is [h] at the start of a native word: hous, hath, hooly, him. It is silent in French loans: honour [ɔˈnuːr], hour, honeste, hoost "host" [ˈɔːst], habit, humble [ˈʊm.blə], heritage.
- wh is [ʍ]: whan [ʍan], which [ˈʍɪtʃ], what.
- th is [ð] at the start of the, that, this, thise, thou, thee, thy, thyn, they, thanne, ther, though, thus, and their forms. It is [ð] between vowels or voiced sounds inside a word: bathed [ˈbaː.ðəd], other, fethere, brother. Elsewhere it is [θ]: thought, thing, with [wɪθ], breeth, heeth, hath, priketh.
- s is [s]. It is [z] between vowels when the modern word has [z]: chese "choose", rose, wise, resoun [rɛˈzuːn], and in the plural houses [ˈhuː.zəs]. The ending -es is [əs], and s at the end of a word is [s]: is [ˈɪs], his [hɪs], was.
- f and v are what is written: lyf [ˈliːf], lyves [ˈliː.vəs], halve [ˈhal.və].
- sh, sch, and ssh are [ʃ]: shoures, schal, fresshe [ˈfrɛ.ʃə]. ch is [tʃ]: chirche [ˈtʃɪr.tʃə], swich [ˈswɪtʃ], March [ˈmartʃ]. j, dg, and g before e, i, y in a French loan are [dʒ]. gg is [dʒ] where the modern word has [dʒ]: brigge [ˈbrɪd.dʒə], egge "edge", segge "say"; elsewhere it is [g.g].
- ng is [ŋ] plus [g] in every position: yonge [ˈjʊŋ.gə], thing [ˈθɪŋg], longen [ˈlɔŋ.gən], kyng. nk is [ŋk]: thanke. n before c, k, g, or qu is [ŋ].
- r is a trill [r] wherever it is written, before a consonant and at the end of a word too: March [ˈmartʃ], flour [ˈfluːr].
- A doubled consonant is a geminate. Write the consonant twice with the syllable boundary between: sonne [ˈsʊn.nə], Aprille [aˈprɪl.lə], croppes [ˈkrɔp.pəs], hadde, lette [ˈlɛt.tə]. ck, sh, and ssh are not geminates: fresshe [ˈfrɛ.ʃə].
- p t k b d g f v θ ð s z ʃ m n l have their ordinary values. There is no aspiration and no dark l to mark.

Syllables
- Every vowel or diphthong makes one syllable. Syllabify each word separately.
- A single consonant between vowels goes with the following vowel. [tʃ], [dʒ], [ʃ], [θ], [ð], [ç], [x], and [kw] each count as one consonant.
- Of two or more consonants, the last goes with the following vowel and the rest close the preceding syllable: perced [ˈpɛr.səd], holt and [ˈhɔlt and], kepte [ˈkɛp.tə].
- A stop or f plus l or r stays together and begins the next syllable: tendre [ˈtɛn.drə], engendred [ɛnˈdʒɛn.drəd], Aprille [aˈprɪl.lə] after its geminate. Any other pair is split: every [ˈɛv.rɪ], palmer [ˈpal.mər].
- [ç] or [x] plus t is a coda: nyght [ˈnɪçt], taughte [ˈtaux.tə].
- A prefix boundary overrides these rules: y-ronne [ɪˈrʊn.nə], bi-gynne.

Stress
- A monosyllable takes ˈ unless it is an article, a preposition, a conjunction, or a possessive: the, a, an, of, to, in, on, at, by, with, for, fro, and, or, but, that, as, if, whan, his, hir, my, thy, oure, youre. that is unstressed also as a relative pronoun.
- A native word stresses the first syllable of its root. The prefixes y-, i-, a-, be-, bi-, for-, to-, and with- are unstressed: yronne [ɪˈrʊn.nə], bigynne, forsake, agoon.
- A French or Latin loan stresses its last full syllable, the last syllable whose vowel is not [ə]: vertu [vɛrˈtiu], licour [lɪˈkuːr], honour [ɔˈnuːr], nature [naˈtiu.rə], corage [kʊˈraː.dʒə], melodye [mɛ.lɔˈdiː.ə], Aprille [aˈprɪl.lə], pilgrimages [pɪl.grɪˈmaː.dʒəs].
- In verse Chaucer often moves the stress of a French loan to its first syllable. The line is iambic: ten syllables, or eleven with a final -e, alternating unstressed and stressed. When the input is verse and the line needs it, stress the first syllable: verray [ˈvɛr.rai], parfit [ˈpar.fɪt], gentil [ˈdʒɛn.tɪl], tendre [ˈtɛn.drə], servyse. Outside verse, use the last full syllable.
- A Latin or Greek name keeps its Latin stress: Zephirus [ˈzɛ.fɪ.rʊs].
- Put ˈ before the stressed syllable. It replaces the dot.
