Map each IPA symbol to the Italian spelling. Write an accent on the stressed vowel of every word that carries ˈ, including a monosyllable. A word with no ˈ gets no accent. /ɪn/, /neː/, and /kʷãː/ have none.

A grave accent marks à, ì, ù, and the open vowels è for /ɛ/ and ò for /ɔ/. An acute accent marks the closed vowels é for /eː/ and ó for /oː/. Open or closed quality is that choice of accent. On a diphthong or a doubled vowel, the accent sits on the first letter: àe, ée. Ordinary Italian spelling marks only a final stressed syllable, and any vowel may carry an accent, so an accent inside the word is legitimate.

The voice lengthens a vowel in exactly one place: a stressed open syllable that is not the last syllable of the word. Stress on the last syllable does not lengthen it. Everywhere else, length is spelled by doubling the letter. A doubled vowel letter is one long vowel, as in cooperare, veemenza, and zii.

One accented letter spells a long vowel that is stressed, in an open syllable, not in the last syllable, and not before the ss written for /s/ between vowels. /aˈmiː.kʊs/ is amìcus.
A doubled letter spells every other long vowel. That is an unstressed long vowel (/ˈka.noː/ is cànoo), a long vowel in a closed syllable (/ˈnuːl.lũː/ is nùullun), a long vowel in a final syllable or a monosyllable (/ˈkʷiː/ is quìi), and a long vowel before that ss.
A short vowel, a nasal vowel, and a diphthong are never doubled.

Vowels
- /a/ /aː/ → a. Stressed, à.
- /ɛ/ → e. Stressed, è.
- /eː/ → e. Stressed, é.
- /ɪ/ /i/ /iː/ /y/ /yː/ → i. Stressed, ì. Italian has no /y/.
- /ɔ/ → o. Stressed, ò.
- /oː/ → o. Stressed, ó.
- /ʊ/ /uː/ → u. Stressed, ù.
- /ãː/ → an. Stressed, àn. /ẽː/ → en. Stressed, én. /ĩː/ → in. Stressed, ìn. /õː/ → on. Stressed, ón. /ũː/ → un. Stressed, ùn. One vowel plus n, never doubled. The n is the second mora.
- /ae̯/ → ae, one syllable. Stressed, àe.
- /oe̯/ → oe, one syllable. Stressed, óe.
- /au̯/ → au, one syllable. Stressed, àu.
- /eu̯/ → eu, one syllable. Stressed, éu.
- /ei̯/ → ei, one syllable. Stressed, éi.
- /ui̯/ → ui, one syllable. Stressed, ùi.

The nasal letter is n, not m. A following consonant pulls an Italian coda n to its own place, as un poco is [um ˈpɔko], so n is the weakest nasal to write.
Italian keeps a, e, and o apart when two of them meet, as in paese and poeta, so ae and oe are read [ae] and [oe], not merged into [e].

Consonants
- /k/ /kʰ/ → c before a, o, u, a consonant, or the end of a word. ch before e or i. e and i soften a bare c, and ch keeps the stop.
- /g/ → g before a, o, u, or a consonant. gh before e or i. e and i soften a bare g, and gh keeps the stop.
- /s/ plus /k/ before e or i → sch. e or i would turn a bare sc into the "sh" sound.
- /ks/ /k.s/ → x, read [ks].
- /kʷ/ → qu, read [kw].
- /gʷ/ → gu, read [gw].
- /w/ → u, the only spelling of [w]. Never v.
- /j/ → i before a vowel.
- /j.j/ → ii. /ˈmaj.jɔr/ is màiior, so the first syllable stays heavy.
- /pʰ/ /tʰ/ → p, t. Plain stops, never f.
- /h/ → nothing. Leave it out. The voice skips every h, and a kept h could form ch or gh.
- /ŋ/ before /k/ or /g/ → n. The voice says [ŋ] when n stands before /k/ or /g/.
- /ŋ.n/ → nn. This is Latin gn. An Italian reader would give gn the palatal /ɲ/.
- /g/ plus /l/ plus /i/ → ghli. The voice reads gli as the palatal /ʎ/.
- /s/ between vowels → ss. A single s between vowels is /z/ in northern-central speech and /s/ in southern-central speech, so a voice may say rosa with [z], which Latin never had. ss guarantees [s], and the consonant is longer. A voice that reliably keeps a single s voiceless writes s instead.
- /s/ elsewhere → s, voiceless at the start, at the end, and next to a voiceless consonant.
- /z/ → z. [dz] is the nearest Italian sound.
- /l/ /ɫ/ → l. Italian has only clear [l].
- /p/ /b/ /t/ /d/ /f/ /m/ /n/ /r/ → the same letters.
- A geminate, the same consonant on both sides of a syllable break, is a doubled letter. /k.k/ is cc or cch, /g.g/ is gg or ggh, and /k.kʷ/ is cqu.

ti before a vowel stays ti. patièntiaa is read with [tj], not the Ecclesiastical [tsj].

A short stressed vowel in an open syllable is lengthened by the voice, as in /ˈka.noː/. For e and o, è against é and ò against ó still separate short from long. For a, i, and u, nothing does.
An unstressed /ɛ/ or /ɔ/ comes out closed. An unstressed syllable has only close [e] and [o].
[h], aspiration, dark /ɫ/, lax /ɪ/ and /ʊ/, and /y/ are lost.
An unstressed i or u before a vowel glides. gàllia loses a syllable to [lja], and pòtuit becomes [twit].
s before a voiced consonant across a word boundary may voice. Italian allows only [z] in that position.
